You're starting a wearables company from scratch. The honest problem with a single large software quote isn't the number, it's that you'd be spending it before you know whether people buy the product. So the proposed approach is to build it the other way around: the smallest build that gets you selling, then the rest once you know there's a customer base for your specific product.

Your first batch is more of a perimeter tester than a money maker. It's there to answer one question: will people buy our product? Everything after that is a decision you get to make once you know the answer.

01
The part that matters most

What you get, in plain terms

When founders say they want to own their product, what they usually mean is something closer to this: they don't want to be trapped, they don't want a competitor getting the same thing, and they don't want the whole company to depend on one person staying interested. Those are the right things to worry about. Here's how each one is answered.

01

Nobody else gets this for child safety. Ever.

Exclusive to FindMe in the business of finding and reuniting children. Not for a year, not while we're working together. Permanently. If a competitor came to me tomorrow with a bag of money, the answer would be no, and it would be in writing.

02

The accounts are in your name, not mine.

The code, the database, and the hosting all live in accounts that belong to FindMe, billed to FindMe. I work inside them as an invited collaborator. That means there's nothing to hand over if we ever part ways, because none of it was ever mine to hold. You remove my access and bring in whoever you want, and the system doesn't so much as blink.

03

It can't be taken away from you.

Permanent and irrevocable. Not a subscription to the software itself, not something that expires, not something I can pull if we have a disagreement. Once a phase is paid for, that phase is yours to use forever.

04

If I disappear, you're fine.

Everything above survives me. If I get hit by a truck, retire, or move to Belize, FindMe keeps operating and you keep every right listed here. You aren't one person's health away from losing your company.

05

If you sell the company, it goes with you.

All of it transfers to a buyer. You aren't building something that turns out to be unsellable because of a clause in a contract with your developer.

02
The trade

What I keep, and why it saves you money

Underneath FindMe there's a general piece of engineering: a way to put a permanent code on a physical object, let one person claim it, and control what a stranger sees when they scan it. That same foundation could just as easily be a luggage tag, a lost pet tag, or a tag on expensive equipment on a job site. None of those compete with you.

So the arrangement is this. You get the finished FindMe product, your brand, your customers, your data, and permanent exclusivity in child safety. I keep the right to use the underlying engineering in areas that have nothing to do with children.

That isn't a technicality. It's the reason the number below is what it is. You're paying to build and own a product rather than to fund a foundation from scratch, and that saves you valuable startup capital.

Yours
  • The FindMe brand, name, and identity
  • Every customer and every customer record
  • All of the data in the system
  • The complete source code, in your own accounts
  • Permanent exclusivity in child safety
  • The right to sell or transfer all of it
Mine
  • The general engineering underneath
  • The right to use it outside of the child safety space
  • Nothing that touches your customers, your data, or your market
One condition, stated up front

Exclusivity is meant to protect an operating business, not to lock up a market forever. So it stays in place as long as FindMe is actually selling. If the company stops selling entirely for an agreed upon number of months, the exclusivity ends while everything else on this page keeps running.

This isn't a trapdoor and it isn't aimed at a slow year. It exists so that if FindMe winds down someday, the arrangement winds down with it.

05
Strongly recommended

Get an attorney to look at this before you launch

The way this is designed already avoids the hardest part of children's privacy law. The child never touches the system and never types anything into it. The parent is the account holder, the parent decides what gets shared, and the only thing stored about a child is a label the parent chose to enter. That's a deliberate design decision, not a happy accident, and it takes most of the usual exposure off the table before you've sold anything.

What it doesn't do is make the question disappear. You'd still be a company holding customer data and selling a product that families rely on in a bad moment, and I'm not an attorney, so I'm not going to tell you where those lines fall. One review before launch is cheap next to finding out later, and I'd feel a lot better if you did it.

The same goes for the agreement between us. Have your own attorney read it. I'd rather you understood it completely than signed it quickly.
